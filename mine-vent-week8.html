Mine Ventilation · Week 8

Sometimes the mine doesn't need more air. It needs colder air.

Push a working mine deep enough and the air itself becomes a hazard — not because it lacks oxygen, but because the body can't shed heat fast enough to survive an 8-hour shift in it. Today: how hot deep mines actually get, how that's measured, and the refrigeration engineering that makes them survivable.

01 · Measuring the hazard

Air temperature alone doesn't tell you the danger.

The standard heat-stress index blends three readings — how hot it feels wet, how hot it feels in radiant heat, and (outdoors) plain air temperature — into one number regulators actually act on.

Indoor / no solar load form
WBGT = 0.7 × T(natural wet-bulb) + 0.3 × T(globe)

02 · What the body is actually protecting

The number that matters is inside the miner, not the air.

WBGT is a proxy. What mining heat-stress programs actually try to control is core body temperature and cardiac strain.

Core temperature

The 38°C ceiling

MSHA's heat stress program targets keeping core body temperature from rising above 38°C (100°F). Medical surveillance triggers above that — 38.0°C for unacclimatized workers, 38.5°C for acclimatized ones — flag a worker for closer monitoring before heat exhaustion or heat stroke sets in.

Ceiling: 38°C · Heat stroke risk climbs sharply above ~40°C
Heart rate

The stop-work rule

A simple field criterion used in mining heat-stress management: if a sustained heart rate minus the worker's age exceeds 180, that's a stop-work signal on its own — no thermometer required, and no waiting for a worse symptom to appear.

Sustained heart rate − age > 180 → stop work
Why this matters WBGT tells you the environment is dangerous. Core temperature and heart rate tell you a specific person is already in trouble in it — and different miners reach that point at very different WBGT levels depending on fitness, hydration, and acclimatization. That's why the tools stack: measure the air, then still watch the miner.
03 · What happens when it fails

One well-documented mine, one very hot summer.

A published one-year case series at this deep underground metal mine recorded 106 heat exhaustion cases — no deaths, but a clear, measurable pattern. The mean WBGT at the time of each case was 31.5°C. Incidence was 3.17 times higher below 1,200 metres than above it, and cases peaked at 147 per million worker-hours during the Australian summer. The study's conclusion was specific enough to design around: heat exhaustion became unlikely once the site's air cooling power exceeded roughly 250 W per square metre.

The lesson: heat exhaustion isn't random — it tracks depth and season closely enough that a mine can set an engineering target (cooling power per square metre) and test whether it's actually working, the same way a ventilation engineer tracks airflow against a design target.

A widely repeated historical account also describes a cluster of heat-stroke deaths at a South African deep gold mine in the 1920s–30s that helped drive early acclimatization protocols — that account comes from a secondary review article, not an independently verified primary investigation, so it's flagged here rather than presented as a confirmed case.

04 · The engineering answer

Cooling the air before it ever goes underground.

Once ventilation alone can't remove heat fast enough, mines add mechanical refrigeration — chilling intake air, or circulating chilled water, at a genuinely industrial scale.

Kidd Creek Mine, Ontario, Canada

One of the world's deepest base-metal mines

Mining between 2,100 m and 3,100 m below surface, with heat problems appearing around 2,300 m. A surface refrigeration plant supplies chilled water at roughly 6°C. Later planning called for a much larger combined system, mixing mechanical plant with a passive "cold stope" that banks winter cold underground.

7.5 MW plant (2004) → planned 19 MW mechanical + 8.5 MW-equivalent passive cooling
Creighton Mine, Sudbury, Canada

Passive cooling banked over decades

At roughly 3 km deep, this mine has run a natural-cooling system since the 1950s, storing winter cold for use underground. A commonly cited mechanical-refrigeration benchmark for comparison: a well-designed plant can produce about 5 units of cooling for every 1 unit of electricity it consumes.

~17 MW from passive/natural cooling · benchmark coefficient of performance ≈ 5
05 · Instrument

How many megawatts does depth actually cost you?

Descending air heats up two ways: autocompression (simple adiabatic heating from air pressure increasing with depth — a fixed, well-measured rate) and heat picked up from the surrounding rock. Field measurements at one deep coal mine found autocompression accounts for 40–60% of the total airway temperature rise measured — the slider below lets you explore that real range.

Heat load = airflow × air density (1.2 kg/m³) × specific heat of air (1.005 kJ/kg·K) × temperature drop needed, a standard sensible-heat calculation. This ignores humidity/latent heat for simplicity — real plants size larger. For scale: Kidd Creek's plant is 7.5–19 MW; a 2012 pre-feasibility study for the Resolution Copper project (Arizona, >2000 m depth) specified roughly 30.5 MW just for underground cooling duty.

06 · The other lever

The body can adapt — on a schedule, not overnight.

Refrigeration isn't the only tool. A graduated exposure schedule lets a new or returning worker's body adapt to heat before it's asked to work a full shift in it.

MSHA's standard graduated schedule for hot sites (WBGT above 26°C / 79°F). South African deep-mine research shows how much engineering and physiology can compress this further — protocols there went from 14 days in the 1950s down to as little as 5 days once climatic-chamber testing and better nutrition science were applied.

08 · Debrief

For the last 15 minutes: talk it through.

In pairs or small groups.

1
Measurement vs. person

WBGT describes the air; core temperature and heart rate describe the person standing in it. If the two disagree — legal WBGT, but a worker's heart rate crossing the stop-work threshold — which one should win, and why might a site's procedures make that harder to act on than it sounds?

2
Engineering economics

Refrigeration at Kidd Creek's or Resolution Copper's scale costs tens of megawatts, continuously, for the life of the mine. At what point does that become a factor in whether a deep ore body gets developed at all, rather than just how it's ventilated?

3
Physics vs. geology

Autocompression is a fixed, predictable rate no matter where you mine. Geothermal heat pickup depends entirely on local rock. What does that split suggest about which parts of a mine's heat problem can be engineered away everywhere, versus which have to be solved site by site?

4
Acclimatization

A graduated schedule protects a new worker but costs productive time. Mount Isa's data shows heat exhaustion incidence more than tripling below 1,200 m — does that change how strictly you'd enforce an acclimatization schedule for workers rotating into the deepest levels specifically, versus everyone else?

5
Career

The Mount Isa study turned "miners are getting heat exhaustion" into a specific, testable engineering target — 250 W/m² of cooling power. As a future engineer, what would it take for you to trust a number like that enough to design around it, versus treating it as one study's result?
