Mine Ventilation · Week 3

Two hundred years ago, a fire did a fan's job.

Before electric fans, mines lit a furnace at the bottom of a shaft on purpose — heat the air, and it rises, dragging fresh air down through the workings behind it. It worked. It also killed people. Today's fans do the same physics, minus the flame sitting in the methane.

FURNACE VENTILATION, c. 1813 — HEAT DRIVES THE CIRCULATION
01 · Then and now

Same physics problem. 200 years apart.

Moving enough air through a mine has always come down to one choice: let buoyancy do the work, or force it with machinery. Both cards below are solving the exact same equation.

1813 · County Durham, England

The Dumb Furnace

Natural ventilation, deliberately amplified
🔥
Power source: open flame

A coal fire built at the base of the upcast shaft heated air, which rose and dragged fresh air down through the mine — the same stack effect you'll calculate below, just deliberately amplified with a flame. It worked. It also put an open fire directly in the path of methane-laden return air, and was, in the words of one historical account, "a frequent cause of explosions." Engineer John Buddle's 1813 "dumb furnace" added a bypass passage so the gassiest air could route around the flame instead of through it — a clever patch on a fundamentally dangerous idea.

Source: North East Bylines, "Ventilation in North East mines 1800–1850."
↓ two centuries later ↓

No flame anywhere near the airstream. Electric motors — 875 kW apiece — spin fans that move roughly 950 m³/s of air, precisely controllable and fully instrumented, heated to a safe minimum before it ever reaches a working face (Week 2). Two hundred years of the same underlying physics problem, solved by removing the danger from the solution instead of routing around it.

02 · Instruments

Two tools. One drives air without machinery, one with it.

Drag the sliders. The second tool is the single most useful chart in a ventilation engineer's career — the operating point is where your fan and your mine actually agree to work.

Aiding the fans — return column is less dense and rises naturally

This is the stack effect, or chimney draft — identical physics to a real chimney, just running the full height of a shaft. A colder, denser intake column and a warmer, lighter return column create a pressure difference with no moving parts at all. In a climate with 78°C of seasonal swing (Week 2), that pressure difference doesn't stay constant — it can flip sign entirely.

03 · Two fan families

Every mine fan is one of two basic shapes.

The chart above works for both — but which family you pick changes what part of the curve you actually get to use.

Axial-flow

Air moves straight through

Blades mounted like a ship's propeller, air enters and exits parallel to the shaft. Compact, and capable of moving huge volumes of air at moderate pressure — the usual choice for a main fan handling hundreds of cubic metres per second, like Oyu Tolgoi's Shaft 2 units.

  • High flow, moderate pressure
  • Smaller footprint for a given airflow
  • Common for main and booster fans
Centrifugal (radial)

Air turns 90° on the way through

Air enters along the shaft and is flung outward by a radial impeller, exiting at right angles — the same basic principle as a squirrel-cage blower. Better suited to high-resistance systems that need more pressure per unit of flow, and generally more tolerant of dust-laden air.

  • Lower flow, higher pressure capability
  • Larger footprint for the same duty
  • Historically common as primary mine fans

05 · Debrief

For the last 15 minutes: talk it through.

In pairs or small groups.

1
Old danger, new form

Furnace ventilation was banned because the fix (heat-driven airflow) sat right next to the hazard (methane) it was supposed to help control. Where else in mine engineering might a safety solution and a hazard end up dangerously close together, the way they were with the furnace?

2
Design philosophy

Mongolia's climate swings from −18°C winters to hot summers. If you were designing a new mine's ventilation there, would you actively design around natural ventilation pressure, or treat it purely as background noise to plan against? Defend your choice.

3
Designing for change

You just saw that series wins at high resistance and parallel wins at low resistance. A mine's actual resistance changes over its life as workings get longer. What does that imply about designing a fan system meant to last 20+ years?

4
Energy economics

Fan affinity laws mean power scales with the cube of speed — small speed increases cost disproportionate energy. When might an engineer choose to add a second fan rather than just spin one fan faster, purely on energy-cost grounds?

5
Career

A 21st-century main fan and an 1813 dumb furnace solve the exact same physics problem. What do you think is the next real change coming to how mines move air — better fans, better automation and data, or something else entirely?
