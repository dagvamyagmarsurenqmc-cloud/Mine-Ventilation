01 · Two mines

One equation, run in opposite directions.

Click a card to see the engineering response. Both are governed by exactly the psychrometric relationships you're about to calculate yourself.

The engineering response

Gobi winters push January mornings to an average of about −18°C. Shaft 2 pulls in roughly 950 m³/s of that frigid air and runs it through glycol heat exchangers fed by water at about 129.5°C from a central heating plant — engineered to guarantee a minimum heater discharge temperature of +2°C through the coldest part of winter.

Notice what the target is not: it isn't a comfortable room temperature. It's the minimum needed to stop ice from cracking shotcrete linings, seizing hoisting equipment, and choking airway cross-sections — heating 950 m³/s any further than that would burn enormous amounts of energy for no safety benefit.

Source: NCCS LLC project documentation, Oyu Tolgoi Shaft 2 mine air heater; climate data from Oyu Tolgoi station records.
The engineering response

At roughly 4 km underground — the deepest workings on Earth — the virgin rock itself sits at about 60°C, and humidity in the workings can exceed 95%. Air this hot and this saturated can barely absorb sweat, which means the human body's main cooling mechanism, evaporation, stops working.

The mine responds by pumping more than 6,000 tonnes of ice slurry underground to chill the ventilating air, backed by large fans to keep that cooled air actually moving past the working faces rather than just sitting there.

Source: Guinness World Records, "Deepest mine" entry.
02 · The four numbers

Every parcel of mine air, described in four numbers.

Everything in this seminar — the heater at Oyu Tolgoi, the ice at Mponeng — comes down to these four properties interacting.

01

Pressure

kPa

Falls with elevation and rises with depth. Ulaanbaatar sits at 1,350 m — its air is already about 15% thinner than sea level before a single fan turns on.

02

Temperature

°C

Set by climate at surface, then reshaped underground by two things working together: compression of descending air, and heat leaking out of the rock itself.

03

Humidity

% RH · kPa vapor pressure

How much water vapor the air is actually carrying versus how much it could carry at that temperature. Governs whether sweat can evaporate at all.

04

Density

kg/m³

Not independent — it falls out of the other three via the ideal gas law. Every fan curve and every ventilation network calculation assumes a density that changes underground.

The tools that turn four numbers into a decision.

The sling psychrometer

Moderate spread — moderately humid air

A sling psychrometer is two thermometers on a handle, one wrapped in a wet wick, spun through the air until the wet bulb stabilizes. Evaporative cooling makes it read lower than the dry bulb — and how much lower tells you the humidity. It's 19th-century technology, and it's still in every mine surveyor's kit bag.

Why moist air floats

Instrument 2 / 3
ρ = Pdry/(RdT) + Pvapor/(RvT)
Dry-air density (same T, P)0.9949 kg/m³
Actual moist-air density0.9833 kg/m³
Moist air is lighter — by 1.16% at these conditions

Water vapor (molar mass 18) is lighter than the nitrogen and oxygen it displaces (average ~29) — so counter-intuitively, humid air is less dense than dry air at the same temperature and pressure. Push the humidity slider to 0% and back to see how much of a fan's rated performance that difference can quietly eat into.

Going down: two ways to get hot

Instrument 3 / 3
T(z) = Tsurface + (dT/dz)·z — evaluated two ways
Air temp from compression alone19.7°C
Rock temp from geothermal gradient47.5°C
Rock is 27.8°C hotter than the compressed air — heat will flow rock → air

Autocompression (≈0.98°C per 100 m) is pure physics — air heats up simply from being compressed as it descends, with zero heat added. It's the same reason a bicycle pump gets warm. The rock's own heat is a separate, usually larger effect once you're several kilometres down. Try the Mponeng preset with a "typical" 25–30°C/km gradient — it wildly overshoots. Real gradient there is much lower, because ultra-deep mines like Mponeng sit in old, thermally "cool" geology. That's not a coincidence — it's part of why 4 km-deep mining is possible there at all.

05 · Debrief

For the last 15 minutes: talk it through.

In pairs or small groups.

1
Design tension

Oyu Tolgoi heats winter air to just above freezing — not to a comfortable temperature. Talk through the tension between worker comfort, equipment protection, and energy cost in that decision. Who should get to make that call, and on what basis?

2
First question

If you were handed a new mine site tomorrow with zero climate data, what's the very first psychrometric question you'd want answered before doing any other design work — and why that one first?

3
Textbook vs. site

A "typical" geothermal gradient badly mis-predicts Mponeng's real temperature. What does that imply about trusting rule-of-thumb numbers versus paying for site-specific measurement, early in a project when budgets are tight?

4
Old tech, new mines

The sling psychrometer is 19th-century technology still riding in modern survey kits alongside digital sensors. Why might a mine still trust the "low-tech" instrument over the digital one in some situations?

5
Career

Mongolia's mines are more likely to face Oyu Tolgoi's cold problem than Mponeng's heat problem — for now. Does that change which half of today's material you'd want to go deeper on, or should a ventilation engineer be equally fluent in both?
