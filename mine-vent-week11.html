01 · The plan has a life after it's approved

Approval isn't the finish line. It's a live document.

Every underground coal mine in the US operates under a written ventilation plan the district manager must approve before a single cut is made under it — and that plan doesn't get filed away. It gets re-examined on a fixed schedule for the life of the mine.

30 CFR §75.370 — current regulatory text
"The ventilation plan for each mine shall be reviewed every 6 months by an authorized representative of the Secretary to assure that it is suitable to current conditions... No proposed ventilation plan shall be implemented before it is approved by the district manager."
— eCFR, Title 30, §75.370
WHO SUBMITS

The operator, in writing

Submitted to the district manager, with miners' representatives notified at least 5 days ahead and given a chance to comment before approval.

WHO CAN PUSH BACK

Either side, on the record

Carbon County Coal Co. (FMSHRC, 1984): the Commission ruled MSHA can't impose generic, one-size-fits-all provisions on a plan — only requirements genuinely suited to that specific mine's conditions.

WHAT'S LIVE RIGHT NOW

The rule is still being rewritten

MSHA proposed a 2025 rule change ("Ventilation Plan Approval Criteria") to limit district managers to only the provisions explicitly listed in §75.371 — narrowing the discretion Carbon County's precedent was fought over.

WHAT HAPPENS IF DENIED

No implementation, period

A rejected plan cannot be run under while contested — the operator must revise and resubmit, or litigate, before mining under new ventilation controls.

Still an open question
The 2025 proposed rule sits at the center of this week's real tension: should a district manager who knows one specific mine's roof, gas, and geology be able to add conditions beyond the codified list — or does that discretion invite exactly the kind of inconsistent, unappealable demands Carbon County Coal Co. pushed back on forty years ago?
02 · How often the inspector actually shows up

The Mine Act sets a hard floor, not a suggestion.

Inspection frequency isn't discretionary — it's written directly into the Federal Mine Safety and Health Act of 1977, and gassier mines get checked far more often than that floor.

30 U.S.C. §813(a)

Underground mines — 4×/year

Every underground coal and metal/nonmetal mine gets a minimum of four complete inspections a year — the same statutory floor for both.

30 U.S.C. §813(a)

Surface mines — 2×/year

Surface coal and metal/nonmetal operations get a minimum of two complete inspections a year under the same section.

30 U.S.C. §813(i)

Gassy mines — spot checks every 5 days

Mines liberating over 1,000,000 ft³ of methane per 24 hours get spot inspections every 5 working days — tiers step down to every 10 or 15 days at lower liberation rates.

UPPER BIG BRANCH, 2010

Frequency means nothing if you know they're coming

Dispatchers were instructed to warn underground crews when inspectors arrived — turning a mandated surprise inspection into a scheduled one. See the case file below.

03 · Three records from the enforcement file

What the paperwork looked like before and after.

One mine gamed the inspection system entirely. One racked up violations fast enough to trigger a rarely-used federal designation. One simply argued its case — and won.

369 citations
Upper Big Branch Mine, West Virginia — April 5, 2010
Illegal advance notice · inadequate air quantities · 29 dead
▾

EXPLOSION29 DEAD$10.8M PENALTY MSHA's post-disaster investigation found the mine's ventilation system produced "erratic changes in air flow and direction," and that no methane examinations were conducted on the longwall tailgate — the ignition point — in the weeks before the explosion. A roof fall, caused by installing only one row of required cable bolts instead of two, further restricted airflow leaving the longwall face, letting methane accumulate.

None of this should have gone undetected for long — except dispatchers testified they were instructed to warn underground miners whenever an MSHA inspector arrived on site, defeating the entire premise of a mandated inspection. The mine's security chief was later criminally convicted for lying to investigators about the practice.

MSHA issued 369 citations and orders to the operator, 21 of them "flagrant," totaling $10,825,368 in penalties — at the time, the largest fine in agency history. A separate global settlement covering related liabilities later reached roughly $209 million.
253 S&S
Brody Mine No. 1, West Virginia — Pattern of Violations, 2013
253 significant-and-substantial violations in 12 months · POV notice · fatalities followed
▾

POV NOTICE253 S&S VIOLATIONS Brody Mining LLC, a Patriot Coal subsidiary, racked up 253 significant-and-substantial violations in the twelve months ending August 2013 — far past either threshold in MSHA's Pattern of Violations rule (30 CFR Part 104), which flags mines exceeding roughly 50–100+ S&S citations in a year depending on which qualifying criteria apply. The mine had also underreported injuries: a later audit turned up 29 unreported injuries and 1,757 lost workdays.

MSHA issued a POV notice in October 2013. Brody's own administrative law judge initially vacated it — but the full Federal Mine Safety and Health Review Commission reversed that decision in September 2015, upholding MSHA's authority to designate the mine.

Two miners were killed at this same mine on May 12, 2014 — after the POV notice, before the Commission's 2015 ruling. A designation meant to force faster correction didn't move fast enough to prevent it.
Operator won
Carbon County Coal Co. — FMSHRC, May 1984
A real limit on how much a district manager can add to a plan
▾

PRECEDENTDUE PROCESS WORKING MSHA tried to require Carbon County's ventilation plan to be built around a fan's "free discharge capacity" rather than the operator's proposed "installed capacity" figure — a generic engineering preference, not something specific to that mine's actual roof, gas, or geology.

The Commission held that MSHA cannot mechanically fold generic guidelines into a plan approval. A provision has to be genuinely "suitable to the conditions and mining system" of that particular mine — anything broader belongs in a formal rulemaking that applies to everyone, with everyone's chance to comment, not a one-off condition slipped into a single plan approval.

This is still the doctrinal basis operators use today to contest an overreaching plan condition — and it's exactly the discretion the 2025 proposed rule is trying to codify away entirely, from the other direction.

07 · Trusting your own measurements

Before you audit the mine, audit your survey.

Every calculator this semester assumed the input numbers were simply correct. In the field, they come from a pressure-quantity survey walked through the actual workings — and every survey carries its own measurement error.

A real, dated field tolerance
"A final result of 3 to 4 feet [of elevation-equivalent pressure error], or 0.04 to 0.06 inch water gage, representing the error of closure, is to be expected" for a closed-loop pressure-traverse survey.
— U.S. Bureau of Mines / MSHA Pittsburgh Health Technology Center, "A Coal Mine Ventilation Survey Method," 1975
There's no tidy percentage to memorize here
Unlike the airflow thresholds above, there is no single, universally-cited "acceptable percent closure error" for a ventilation network survey across the field's major references — this 1975 figure is a specific absolute tolerance for one particular survey technique, not a general standard. The report itself warns that a small closure error can still hide compensating mistakes, which is why real practice leans on independent check-loops rather than trusting one number.

09 · Debrief

For the last 15 minutes: talk it through.

In pairs or small groups.

1
Discretion vs. codification

The 2025 proposed rule would limit district managers to only the provisions codified in §75.371. Carbon County Coal Co. shows discretion can be abused — but does removing it entirely trade one problem for a worse one?

2
Is the penalty enough?

The maximum regular civil penalty for a single violation is $90,649. Is that a meaningful deterrent for a large multinational operator the same way it might be for a small one — and should the formula account for that difference more directly than it does?

3
Enforcement speed

Brody Mine received its Pattern of Violations notice in October 2013. Two miners died there in May 2014, before the notice was even upheld on appeal. What would it take for an enforcement designation to change conditions in months rather than years?

4
Defeating the inspection itself

Upper Big Branch's advance-notice scheme meant the entire inspection frequency requirement was pointless in practice. With today's tracking and communication systems (Week 10), what would make an inspection actually unannounced again?

5
Closing the semester

Looking back across every calculator this term — dilution, network solving, booster reach, dust, heat, governing criteria, escape time, refuge sizing, and now compliance — which single number would you check first if you were the inspector walking into an unfamiliar mine, and why?

Closing this arc
This closes the semester's run from first-principles design through emergency response to regulatory audit. The ventilation system was never just a network of fans and airways — it was always also the paperwork, the inspection schedule, and the penalty table standing behind it, whether or not anyone in the mine ever thinks about them on an ordinary shift.
