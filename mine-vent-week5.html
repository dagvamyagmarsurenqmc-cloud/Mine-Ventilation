01 · The analogy — and where it breaks

Almost exactly an electrical circuit. Almost.

Mine ventilation networks obey the same continuity logic as a DC circuit. The one difference changes everything about how you solve them.

Electrical circuit

Ohm's Law

V = I · R

Voltage drop is linear in current. Double the current, double the voltage drop. Junction currents balance (Kirchhoff's current law). Networks solve with straightforward linear algebra.

VS
Mine ventilation network

The Square Law

P = R · Q²

Pressure drop is quadratic in airflow — a legacy of turbulent flow. Junction airflows balance exactly like current does. But that squared term means the system is nonlinear.

Why this matters A linear network (Ohm's Law) solves in one pass of algebra. A quadratic network doesn't — which is exactly why mine ventilation engineers borrowed an iterative method, originally built for something else entirely, to solve networks by successive correction instead of direct solution.
02 · Borrowed math

The method wasn't built for mines at all.

In 1930, structural engineer Hardy Cross published a method for finding forces in statically indeterminate buildings — trusses and frames with more supporting members than simple equilibrium equations could solve directly. In November 1936, he re-published the same core idea for an entirely different problem: flow distribution in networks of pipes.

The trick in both cases is the same: guess a starting distribution, calculate how badly it violates the network's balance conditions, apply a correction, and repeat until the errors shrink to nothing. Mine ventilation engineers adopted this exact approach for airway networks — guess an airflow distribution, check it against the square law at every loop, correct, repeat. It's the direct ancestor of the network-solving software mines actually use today.

03 · Instrument 1

Where a single airway's resistance actually comes from.

Every R you'll use today is built from four numbers describing one airway's geometry and surface roughness.

The Atkinson equation

Single airway
R = (k · per · L) / A³
Airway resistance R0.0486
Try doubling area alone — watch what happens to R

Friction factor k depends on lining and roughness — smooth concrete-lined shafts sit toward the low end, rough unlined rock toward the high end; these sliders are illustrative, and real designs use site-measured or tabulated values. Notice the exponent: R depends on the cube of area. That single exponent is why "make the airway bigger" is usually a far more powerful lever than almost anything else available to a ventilation engineer.

Solve the whole network at once.

Two development headings (R1, R2) run in parallel, then rejoin into one return airway (R3) back to the fan — the exact layout in the hero diagram. Watch how airflow splits unevenly, and how one branch changes the whole system's operating point.

Parallel resistances don't add like electrical resistors — they combine as 1/√R_eq = 1/√R1 + 1/√R2, a direct consequence of the square law. Push R2 higher (simulating a partially blocked or damaged heading) and watch Heading 1 absorb more and more of the total flow — air always takes the path that costs it least pressure, exactly like current, just with a different exponent.

06 · Debrief

For the last 15 minutes: talk it through.

In pairs or small groups.

1
Systems thinking

A single blocked or damaged airway doesn't just lose its own airflow — it changes the flow through every other connected airway. What does that imply about how a ventilation engineer should prioritize maintenance across a whole network, not just airway by airway?

2
Engineering economics

Resistance scales with the cube of area — double the area, resistance drops by a factor of eight. What does that suggest about "make the airway bigger" versus "add more fan power" as two different responses to the same airflow shortfall?

3
Cross-disciplinary

Hardy Cross borrowed a method invented for analyzing forces in buildings and applied it to airflow in pipes two years later. What other engineering ideas do you think could cross over between subfields the way this one did?

4
Hazard

Lower-resistance paths always win more airflow. What real hazard could that create for a working face that becomes even slightly harder to ventilate than the paths around it?

5
Career

Modern mines use ventilation network software instead of manual Hardy Cross iteration. Does understanding the manual method still matter if software does it for you? Defend your answer.
