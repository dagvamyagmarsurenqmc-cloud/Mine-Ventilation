Mine Ventilation · Week 9

Every calculator this semester was really one question, asked six different ways.

How much air does this actually need? Diesel exhaust, methane, dust, heat, minimum velocity — each has its own formula, and you've now used all of them. Planning a mine's ventilation system means running every one of those numbers and building around whichever comes back largest. Not the sum. The largest.

01 · The one rule underneath everything

You don't add the requirements. You take the biggest one.

It's tempting to think a mine needs "diesel air plus gas air plus dust air plus heat air." It doesn't — because the same cubic metre of air dilutes diesel fumes, carries away methane, clears dust, and removes heat all at once. Provide enough for the hungriest single criterion, and the others come along for free.

Source text
"The minimum dilution air flow rate is determined by the contaminant requiring the greatest dilution quantity, with due cognizance of the possible additive effects of mixtures and synergism..."
— International Labour Organization, Encyclopaedia of Occupational Health and Safety, "Ventilation and Cooling in Underground Mines"
Criterion 1
Diesel equipment

Airflow to dilute diesel exhaust gases and particulate to safe concentrations, scaled to installed engine power.

Criterion 2
Gas dilution

Airflow to dilute methane or other gas make below a statutory limit — the same formula from Week 1.

Criterion 3
Minimum velocity

A floor on air speed through any heading, regardless of what's being diluted — simple area times velocity.

Criterion 4
Heat removal

Airflow to carry away heat load fast enough to hold a target temperature rise — the same physics as Week 8.

Watch how close diesel and heat run at the defaults: nudge the heat load up slightly and the governing criterion flips entirely, which is exactly the kind of sensitivity a real planning study has to check before committing to a fan and shaft size.

03 · The number that never goes away

Ventilation is usually a mine's single biggest electricity bill.

Once the system is sized and built, it runs continuously for the life of the mine — and that adds up to a startling share of total operating cost.

04 · Planning done well

Two mines that planned for what came next.

One of the world's deepest base-metal mines (workings run to roughly 3,000 m) installed a ventilation-on-demand system: RFID cap-lamp tags and occupancy sensors feed a central controller that adjusts fan speed and louvre position in real time, based on where people and equipment actually are — instead of running every fan at full capacity, everywhere, all the time. The result, independently published by Ontario's electricity system operator: roughly $2 million per year in energy savings, paying back the investment in about two years. One targeted fan upgrade alone — replacing two 200-horsepower fans with twelve much smaller 3-horsepower units — cut electricity use on that system by 98%.

The lesson: a well-planned ventilation system isn't just sized correctly once — it's built so it can be run efficiently for the rest of the mine's life, not just powerful enough to survive its worst day.

As Palabora's underground block cave ("Lift I") approached depletion, the mine didn't wait for it to run out before planning what came next. "Lift II" is being developed 450 metres deeper, targeting production out to roughly 2040 — and a dedicated new ventilation shaft was sunk to 1,200 m depth specifically to serve it, holed through in early 2024. A new quadrafurcated fan station with four 3-megawatt fans, each moving about 400 m³/s, adds 1,200 m³/s of fresh ventilating air for the deeper phase alone.

South Africa's South Deep mine shows the same staged logic even further along: as it deepened toward 3,000 m, it added a twin-shaft ventilation system delivering 1,200 m³/s of cooled air, backed by a surface ice plant and an underground refrigeration plant — the same refrigeration engineering from Week 8, planned in from the start of the deepening phase rather than bolted on afterward.

The lesson: ventilation capacity has to be planned in phases tied to the mine's own depth roadmap — sizing only for today's workings guarantees a much more expensive retrofit later.

07 · Debrief

For the last 15 minutes: talk it through.

In pairs or small groups.

1
Design logic

"Take the largest requirement, not the sum" only works because the same air serves every purpose at once. Can you think of a situation in mine ventilation where that assumption might not hold — where two criteria could actually compete for the same air rather than share it?

2
Sensitivity

At today's calculator defaults, diesel and heat were separated by less than 15% of the governing value. If you were the engineer, would you design to today's governing criterion, or build in margin against whichever one is running second? What would decide that for you?

3
Economics

Ventilation can be a quarter to half of a mine's entire electricity bill, yet it's often planned mainly around worst-case safety scenarios. How should a ventilation engineer balance "big enough for the worst day" against "efficient enough for every ordinary day"?

4
Staged planning

Palabora sank a new shaft before Lift I ran out, rather than after. What organizational or financial pressures do you think make it hard for a mine to commit that kind of capital early, even when the engineering case is clear?

5
Synthesis

Looking back across the whole semester — gases, psychrometrics, fans, networks, dust, heat — which single topic do you think most changes how you'd approach planning a brand-new mine's ventilation system from scratch, and why?
