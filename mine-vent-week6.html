Mine Ventilation · Week 6

A big fan at the shaft does nothing for the last few hundred metres.

Every heading starts as a dead end. Whatever air reaches the face has to be pushed there through a duct, one joint and one leak at a time — and the further that duct runs, the more the main fan's effort gets eaten before it arrives. Today: what actually gets lost along the way, and when one fan simply isn't enough.

01 · Three ways to duct one heading

Push it, pull it, or both.

Every auxiliary ventilation system is a variation on three basic layouts. The choice isn't about airflow quantity alone — it's about where the dust and gas end up relative to the people working there.

Forcing

Blower system

A duct carries fresh air from the fan straight to the face. Contaminated air finds its own way back through the open heading. Simple, cheap, and the fastest way to dilute gas right at the face — which is why gassy mines often mandate it as the only permitted method.

+ Fastest gas dilution at the face

− Dust and fumes travel back over the crew

Exhaust

Extraction system

Fresh air moves up the open heading on its own; a duct pulls contaminated air away from near the face. Workers stand in cleaner air on the way in. It only works if the duct inlet is actually capturing the dirty air — get the position wrong and pockets of gas simply sit still.

+ Crew works in fresher air

− Depends entirely on duct-inlet placement

Overlap

Combined system

Both ducts run at once, overlapping near the face. Gets close to the best of both — but the exhaust leg has to pull noticeably harder than the forcing leg pushes, or contaminated air recirculates straight back into the fresh supply instead of leaving.

+ Closest to "best of both"

− Exhaust must run ~30% higher than forcing, or it backfires

02 · When the duct fails

Three case files.

One is about a fan simply being switched off. One is about a curtain hung in the wrong place. One is about the fan itself becoming the fire.

An underground booster fan was being stopped so another fan could be disconnected. To do it, workers closed the fan's bypass door — the door meant to let air keep flowing around a stopped fan. With it shut, ventilation to part of the mine simply stopped. Firedamp (methane) built up in the dead air and was ignited by electrical equipment. Six men died.

The lesson regulators drew: a booster fan's bypass door must open automatically the instant the fan stops — never rely on someone remembering.

A belt-driven underground booster fan overheated and caught fire. The roadway it sat in was lined with combustible lagging, which let the fire spread far faster than anyone could respond. Forty-seven men died — almost all from carbon monoxide, trapped by smoke in a mine with no other way out of that section. It remains one of Scotland's worst mining disasters.

The lesson: a fan burning underground isn't a ventilation failure, it's an ignition source — which is exactly why coal-mining regulators now treat booster fans as explosion-protected equipment, not ordinary machinery.

The mine's plan permitted this single-entry heading to be driven a maximum of 65 feet unventilated before line curtain had to reach the face. It was actually driven 377 feet. Investigators later found methane above 5% and carbon monoxide above 1,500 ppm in the unventilated section. A shot fired without proper stemming ignited the accumulated gas. One miner, age 43, was killed.

The lesson: leakage and misplacement aren't abstract inefficiencies — "the duct isn't quite reaching the face" is the exact gap where gas has room to accumulate.
03 · Instrument 1

Most of what the fan pushes never reaches the face.

Every joint, seam, and worn patch along a duct leaks a little. Over hundreds of metres, "a little" adds up to a lot. These percentages are field-measured industry rules of thumb, not a universal formula — always check the actual delivered airflow at the face.

Delivered-to-face estimator

Fan supplies 20 m³/s at the duct inlet
Estimated leakage35%
Airflow reaching the face13.0 m³/s
Lost along the duct7.0 m³/s

Ranges drawn from Le Roux / Holtzhausen field-derived benchmarks: flexible duct commonly loses 10–55% over its length depending on condition; well-maintained rigid steel duct loses as little as 2–25%. Notice how much the "good vs. poor" toggle moves the result at the same length — installation discipline is often a bigger lever than duct material.

04 · Instrument 2

How far can one fan actually reach?

Remember the cube law from last week — resistance depends on airway area cubed? It's back, and this time it decides whether a single auxiliary fan can still ventilate the face, or whether the heading has grown too long without a second fan in series.

Fan curves and duct friction factor here follow the same P₀(1−(Q/Qmax)²) and Atkinson R=k·per·L/A³ forms used in Weeks 3 and 5; a booster simply adds its own pressure in series with the main fan's, the same way two batteries in series add voltage.

05 · The catch

Why coal mines are wary of the exact fix that helps.

A booster fan solves the long-duct problem cleanly. It also concentrates real risk at one point deep underground — which is exactly what Oxcroft and Auchengeich were about.

If it stops

Bypass, or it's a dead end behind a dead fan

Modern regulation (e.g. NSW's coal mine ventilation guidance) requires a booster fan's bypass door to open automatically the instant the fan stops or loses power — with remote monitoring of the door position itself. Oxcroft's door was closed by hand and stayed that way.

If it fails

Explosion-protected, continuously monitored

Booster fan motors and electrics in gassy coal mines are required to be explosion-protected, with continuous gas monitoring that stops non-protected equipment above roughly 0.25% methane, plus vibration and temperature trips. Auchengeich's fan was neither protected nor sitting in a non-combustible roadway.

Coal vs. metal mines

The same fan, very different rulebook

Underground metal and non-metal mines use booster fans comparatively routinely — without methane and coal dust in the air, a stopped or failed fan is a productivity problem, not an explosion risk. In coal, the same piece of equipment sits inside an entirely different, far stricter regulatory regime.

Why not just skip boosters

Because the alternative is a longer, weaker duct

Ban booster fans outright and the only remaining lever is a bigger single fan or a bigger duct — both with real cost and physical limits. The regulatory answer isn't "never use one," it's "use one, engineered so its failure mode can't become the next Auchengeich."

Why this matters A booster fan is a real, standard engineering answer to the reach problem in today's second calculator. It is also machinery running unattended, underground, for years — which is precisely why the regulatory response to two fan disasters four decades apart was "make the failure mode safe," not "make the fan illegal."

08 · Debrief

For the last 15 minutes: talk it through.

In pairs or small groups.

1
Design tradeoff

Forcing systems dilute gas fastest at the face but send dust and fumes back over the crew; exhaust systems protect the crew but depend on precise duct placement. If you had to pick one default for a gassy coal heading versus a dusty (but gas-free) metal heading, would your choice differ? Why?

2
Risk transfer

A booster fan solves a real airflow problem but creates a new failure point deep underground. Is this a trade you'd always take if the alternative is "the face doesn't get enough air," or are there situations where the answer should be no?

3
Regulation

Oxcroft (1919) and Auchengeich (1959) are forty years apart, yet both are booster-fan disasters. What does that gap suggest about how long it takes an industry to actually absorb a safety lesson — and what would you want to see to feel confident the lesson has finally stuck?

4
Economics

Reducing leakage (better duct, tighter joints) and adding a booster fan both increase delivered airflow. One is a maintenance discipline problem, the other is a capital equipment decision. Which would you reach for first, and what would change your answer?

5
Hazard

The R&D Coal Company case wasn't a broken fan — it was a plan that permitted 65 feet, and a heading driven to 377. What does that tell you about where the real risk in auxiliary ventilation usually sits: the equipment, or the discipline around it?
